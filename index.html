Part 2 · Learning objective 1

What the retailer actually does

In a textbook chain, the retailer is the last stop before the customer: it buys finished goods and puts them on a shelf. Build-A-Bear stretches that role in four directions.

The experience

It turns a product into an event

The bear arrives as an empty skin. Customers pick it, stuff it, add a heart, dress it and name it. About one-third of revenue is tied to birthdays or parties. The store does work no factory can do, which is why customers come in instead of clicking “buy.”

Vertical

It owns most of the chain

Build-A-Bear designs its products, orders them from contract factories, runs its own 350,000 sq ft distribution center in Groveport, Ohio, and sells through its own stores and website. It acts as producer, distributor and retailer at once.

Channel captain

It sets the rules for partners

Only 375 of its 662 locations are company-run. The rest are run by partners (in theme parks, cruise ships and other stores) or by international franchisees who buy goods from Build-A-Bear. It decides what they sell and how the experience works, the role slide 5 of the deck calls the channel captain.

~40%

It reads its customers

Teens and adults (“kidults”) make up roughly 40% of customers. Licenses like Pokémon, Sanrio and Disney pull them in. Each season it orders more of what sells and drops what doesn't, using store and loyalty-club data to decide.

Part 3 · Learning objective 2

Leaving China, one factory at a time

Before 2020, more than 90% of Build-A-Bear's merchandise came from China. Rising tariffs and pandemic shutdowns showed how risky that was. By fiscal 2025, China's share was down to about half, and Vietnam had taken most of the rest.

Share of merchandise by source country

China Vietnam

The company reports pre-2020 China sourcing as “over 90%,” shown here at 90. No Vietnam figure is reported for that period. Sources: Build-A-Bear FY2025 10-K; Bloomberg Law (FY2024 split).

Why this matters: moving to Vietnam spreads the risk across two countries, but not far. Five vendors supply 75% of all merchandise, up from 69% a year earlier, and every order is placed purchase order by purchase order with no long-term contract. That gives Build-A-Bear flexibility, but it also means no supplier is promised anything in return, which is the opposite of the trust-based partnerships the deck describes on slide 4.

Part 4 · Trade policy whiplash

Eighteen months of tariff swings

A retailer that orders a year ahead has to guess what import taxes will be when the goods arrive. Between 2025 and 2026, those guesses kept changing.

  1. April 2025US tariffs on Chinese goods hit at least 145%.Build-A-Bear warns investors that tariffs are a “significant risk to our business operations.”
  2. Mid 2025Rates settle near 30% on China and 20% on Vietnam.Management says the year's tariff cost will stay under $11M, offset partly with selective price increases.
  3. FY2025 closeRecord year anyway: $529.8M revenue.But pre-tax margin slips from 13.5% to 12.7% as about $11M of tariff costs eat into profit.
  4. Feb 20, 2026The Supreme Court strikes down the IEEPA tariffs.New tariffs are announced under other laws. Build-A-Bear calls the outlook “highly uncertain.”
  5. Q1 FY2026A $13.2M tariff refund is booked.The money comes back with interest by the second quarter.
  6. Q2 FY2026Revenue falls 7% and pre-tax income falls 24%.Gross margin drops from 57.6% to 54.2%. Guidance for the full year includes about $16M of tariff costs at a 10% rate.

Part 5 · Using data against a long lead time

Ordering a year ahead, adjusting every week

It takes about 12 months to move a bear from concept to store shelf, including 90 to 150 days from the start of production to delivery. The deck's slide 3 points out that long lead times force a retailer to hold more inventory. They also set up the bullwhip effect: a small surprise in what customers want turns into large swings in orders further up the chain, because everyone is ordering on old information.

Build-A-Bear uses its own data to shrink that gap:

Reorder the winners

Assortments are updated by buying more of the top-selling styles and cutting weaker ones, so later orders follow real sales instead of the original forecast.

Stores as mini warehouses

Online orders can ship from a store or be picked up in one. Inventory sitting in any store can fill demand anywhere, which means less extra stock is needed.

Know the customer

The Bonus Club loyalty program and online drops aimed at adults show which licenses and styles each group wants before the next big order is placed.

The store still wins

Online demand fell 5.5% in fiscal 2025 while total revenue hit a record. Most sales still happen in person, which supports the idea that the in-store experience, not the website, is the competitive edge.

Part 7 · Analysis

What this case shows

Build-A-Bear's strength is the part of the chain it controls: the store. Its weakness is the part it doesn't: factories and trade policy on the other side of the world.

On the retailer's role (LO1): the retailer here is not just the last stop where goods are sold. It designs the product, controls distribution, sets the rules for its partners, and creates value the factory can't by finishing the product with the customer. That's why it posted five straight record years while much of mall retail struggled.

On global challenges (LO2): concentrating production in China exposed it to tariffs; moving to Vietnam helped but kept it in the same region. A 12-month lead time means it commits to orders before it knows the tariff rate, and relying on five vendors without contracts means one disruption hits a large share of its stock. The tariff swings of 2025–26 show up directly in its profit margin.

Three recommendations

Deepen supplier partnerships

Move its five key vendors from order-by-order buying to longer agreements with shared forecasts (CPFR, slide 4), so it gets priority when capacity is tight.

Diversify beyond Asia

Test a third sourcing region closer to North America to cut lead time and spread tariff risk across more than two countries.

Use data earlier in the chain

Feed loyalty-club and store sales data into the first order, not just reorders, so the 12-month bet is based on better information.
